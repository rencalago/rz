Pintados-Kasadyaan Festival Tacloban: June Visitor Guide

Ren Calago 25 October 20260

Pintados-Kasadyaan is Tacloban's June festival, and the days that matter most for a visitor are the last few of the month. The ritual dance showdown usually lands somewhere between June 27 and 29, and the city fiesta for Señor Santo Niño de Tacloban follows on June 30. Aim for that stretch, book a room early, and leave room in your plans for a schedule that can change from one year to the next.


What makes this festival different from the big January ones is the look of the dancers. Performers cover their arms, legs, chest and face in painted patterns that copy the tattoos of Visayan warriors from centuries ago, and they share the field with dance groups from towns across Leyte, each carrying its own hometown festival.


This guide explains the two halves of the name, how the June date came about, what happens on competition day, where to sit, and the practical details that make a festival trip to Tacloban easier.




One festival, two names


Pintados is the Tacloban City half. The word means "painted," and it is what Spanish chroniclers called the Visayans they met in the 16th century, many of them tattooed from head to foot. On a warrior, those marks recorded courage in battle and standing in the community. Today's dancers recreate them with body paint, often in designs that look like armour.


Kasadyaan is the Leyte province half. It is the Waray word for merriment, and in practice it means a Festival of Festivals: contingents from different towns bring their own local celebrations to one stage. The Buyogan Festival of Abuyog, the Lingganay Festival of Alangalang and the Karatong Festival of Dulag are among the ones that have taken part.


The full official name is the Leyte Pintados-Kasadyaan Festival. Most people simply say Pintados.


Why Tacloban celebrates the Santo Niño in June


Many Philippine cities honour the Santo Niño in January. Tacloban moved its fiesta to June 30, a date the city links to the return of its Santo Niño image in 1889 and the end of a cholera outbreak that year. June 30 has stayed the patronal fiesta ever since.


The festival came much later. In 1986, local business and civic leaders set up the Pintados Foundation, Inc. to build a cultural event around Leyte's tattooed heritage and the devotion to the Santo Niño, and the first Pintados Festival was held on June 29, 1987. The provincial Kasadyaan gathering was later joined to it, which is how one event ended up with two names.


The format has not been fixed. After a pause during the pandemic, the full festival returned in 2024 at the Leyte Sports Development Center with around ten contingents. In 2025, organisers postponed the Festival of Festivals competition after only three towns confirmed participation following the May elections, though the city's fiesta activities went ahead. That history is the best reason to check the official pages before you book anything.


What you'll see on festival week


The painted warriors


This is the picture most people come for. Expect masks, headdresses made with feathers and abaca, and paint in black, blue, gold and white. The choreography mixes warrior movement, folk dance and theatre, with drums and agung gongs setting the pace. Each group tells a story through its routine, which might be a pre-colonial legend, a day in a fishing village or a local hero.


The Festival of Festivals showdown


Each contingent gets roughly 15 to 20 minutes on the field. Judges look at choreography, costumes, music and theme, and the crowd makes no secret of which town it came to support. Some of the stronger groups go on to compete in national festival contests, so the production values can be high, with big props and live chanters.


The street parade, in some years


When permits and weather allow, contingents dance through downtown Tacloban in the morning before the ritual showdown, starting and ending at the Grandstand. In other years everything stays inside the sports complex. The route, when there is one, is usually announced only a few days ahead on the city's social media pages.


The rest of June at RTR Plaza


The Pintados-Kasadyaan Festival Park, better known as RTR Plaza, hosts activities for most of the month: evening shows, food stalls, trade fairs, garden and pet shows, and pageants such as Mutya ng Kasadyaan and Miss Pintados. The Santo Niño Church on Real Street draws devotees all month, and the June 30 fiesta Mass is the high point for many local families.


Where to watch


The main venue for the showdown is the Leyte Sports Development Center, which locals call the Grandstand. In recent years the performances have started around 1 or 2 in the afternoon. Covered seats go first, so arriving two hours early is reasonable if you want shade. Bring water, a hat and a small fan, because the afternoon sun is harsh even in the covered sections.


Entry has often been free or controlled through tickets handed out by the organisers, and some sections are reserved for invited guests. Ask at the Tacloban City Information Office or your hotel's front desk a day before to find out where tickets, if any, are being given out that year.


If a parade is announced, stake out a spot along Imelda Avenue or whichever streets the published route names. For a slower evening after the main event, RTR Plaza is free and easy to wander.


Getting to Tacloban and moving around


Daniel Z. Romualdez Airport has daily flights from Manila and Cebu, and the ride into downtown takes about 15 to 20 minutes by taxi or van. Fares climb in the last week of June as residents fly home for the fiesta, so book as soon as the festival dates are out. Buses and vans also reach the city from Ormoc and from Samar across the San Juanico Bridge. If you have time to spare before or after, our San Juanico Bridge and Tacloban plan covers the city's other sights.


Inside the city, jeepneys, multicabs and tricycles handle short hops. Roads near the Grandstand are often closed or rerouted on competition day, so give yourself extra time and expect to walk the last stretch.


Practical tips before you go


Rooms in downtown Tacloban fill up around June 28 to 30. If the city is booked out, look at Palo, the next town south, which is a short drive away.


Late June means heat, humidity and sudden rain. A light poncho works better than an umbrella in packed stands, where an open umbrella blocks the people behind you. If you're watching a street parade, keep your phone or camera in a bag you can close quickly, since paint and water tend to fly around.


Carry enough cash for food and transport, because ATM queues get long during fiesta week. Lunch at a downtown carinderia is usually calmer than the evening crowd at the plaza stalls. Look out for moron, the Leyte rice cake swirled with chocolate, which makes an easy snack to take into the stands.


Remember that the festival is a religious celebration first. Dress modestly for church, and step aside rather than cut through a procession.


Making a longer Leyte trip of it


Two days in Tacloban is enough to see the showdown on one day and the fiesta and park on the next. With more time, June is still workable for the region's outdoor trips. The Biliran waterfalls are a few hours north by road, and the Sohoton and Calbiga caves in Samar are reached from Tacloban across the bridge, though rain can affect both, so check conditions before committing.


Is Pintados-Kasadyaan worth planning around?


It is if you care more about a festival that still feels local than about polish and scale. Pintados is smaller than Sinulog or Ati-Atihan, and some years bring a full street parade while others keep everything inside the stadium. What stays constant is the painted dancers, the drumlines and a stadium full of towns cheering for their own.


Watch the Tacloban City Government and Pintados-Kasadyaan Facebook pages from early June, confirm the final schedule a week before you fly, and get to the Grandstand early enough to claim some shade.
