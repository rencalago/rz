# Island-Hopping Safety and What to Bring

Island-hopping is the core activity in Palawan, Cebu, Bohol, Siargao, and Boracay — small bangka boats, open water, sun, and multiple stops in one day. Most trips are uneventful, but safety depends on checking weather, wearing life vests correctly, and packing for sun and water rather than assuming the boat provides everything.

Boats in the Philippines are regulated by the Philippine Coast Guard, which can cancel trips when winds or waves exceed safe limits. Operators who skip life vests or overload boats risk fines and suspension. As a traveler, your best safety tools are simple: ask about conditions before boarding, wear your vest properly on the boat, and keep your gear dry and protected.

Here is what to bring, what the safety rules actually say, and how to make a typical island-hopping day smoother.

## 1. Safety Rules You Should Know Before Boarding

**Life vests are mandatory on boats:** Philippine Coast Guard regulations require all motorized boats carrying passengers to have life vests for everyone on board, and passengers must wear them while the boat is moving. Boats caught without enough vests or with passengers not wearing them can be fined and suspended from operating for days.

**Coast Guard can cancel trips:** If PAGASA issues a gale warning or sea conditions are rough, the Coast Guard suspends small boat operations. This happens most often June to October during Habagat, but also during Amihan surges December to February. If your operator says trips are cancelled due to Coast Guard advisory, it is not a negotiation tactic — it is enforced at the port.

**Overloading and boat condition:** Licensed bancas have a maximum capacity painted on the hull. Count heads — if the boat looks overloaded or has no visible life vests, choose another operator. Larger, well-maintained boats with a roof handle waves better than small open boats.

**Best for:** All island-hopping, especially first-timers and families.

**What to do:** Before you pay, ask: Is there a Coast Guard clearance today? How many life vests are on board? What is the max capacity? A good operator answers directly and shows vests without being asked.

## 2. What to Wear and What to Pack

Pack for sun, water, and quick transfers between boat and beach.

**Wear:**
- **Rash guard or long-sleeve swim shirt:** Better than heavy sunscreen and protects from sun and jellyfish. Light-colored dries faster.
- **Swimwear underneath:** Quick-drying.
- **Reef shoes or sturdy sandals:** For rocky entries, sea urchin areas, and boat ladders. Flip-flops are fine but slip on wet decks.
- **Hat and sunglasses with strap:** Sun is strong even on cloudy days. A strap keeps sunglasses from falling into the sea.

**Bring in a small daypack or dry bag:**
- **Dry bag 10–20 liters:** The single most useful item. Keeps phone, cash, and camera dry. Boats splash.
- **Reef-safe sunscreen:** Choose mineral-based without oxybenzone and octinoxate. Apply before boarding, not in the water.
- **Towel or sarong:** Quick-dry towel or sarong that doubles as shade.
- **Water — at least 1 liter per person:** Most tours provide small bottles, but not enough in heat. Bring your own.
- **Snacks:** Crackers or fruit for longer tours.
- **Snorkel gear if you have it:** Rental masks often leak. If you bring your own mask and snorkel, you get a better seal. Fins are optional for island-hopping — many stops are shallow.
- **Phone in waterproof pouch:** For photos and emergency contact, but keep it inside the dry bag when not in use.
- **Cash in small bills:** Environmental fees, terminal fees, small island vendor fees, and tips are cash-only. PHP 20, 50, 100 bills are easiest.
- **Basic meds:** Seasickness tablets (take 30 minutes before boarding), plasters, antihistamine if you are prone to jellyfish stings.
- **ID and hotel address:** Written on paper, not just on phone.

**What not to bring:** Large hard suitcases, single-use plastic bottles where prohibited, heavy drones without permits (many marine sanctuaries ban drones), and valuables you cannot afford to get wet.

## 3. On the Boat — How to Stay Safe During the Day

**Sit low and centered:** Bancas are narrow. Sit near the center and low when seas are choppy. Avoid sitting on the outrigger arms.

**Wear your vest on the boat, adjust for snorkeling:** Wear the life vest snugly while the boat moves. For snorkeling, some guides require you to keep it on, others allow you to remove it if you are a confident swimmer — follow the guide and local rules. If you keep it on while snorkeling, adjust it so you float horizontally, not vertically.

**Hydrate and watch the sun:** Dehydration and sunburn are more common than boat incidents. Drink water regularly, reapply reef-safe sunscreen on face and shoulders, and use your sarong as shade during midday stops.

**Follow sanctuary rules:** Most island-hopping stops are marine protected areas. Do not step on coral, do not touch turtles, do not feed fish, and stay inside buoy lines. Guides can end a swim if rules are broken — this is enforced at Balicasag, Apo Island, and El Nido lagoons.

**Seasickness:** If you are prone, take medication before boarding and sit at the back where motion is less. Look at the horizon. Avoid heavy, oily breakfast before an early departure.

**Weather changes:** Afternoon thunderstorms are common in wet season. If your boatman suggests heading back early, listen — return trips into building waves are harder than going out.

## 4. Choosing a Good Island-Hopping Operator

**Licensed and registered:** Look for a boat with a Coast Guard sticker, a visible name, and a manifest sheet. Registered operators at Alona Beach, El Nido, Coron, and Siargao usually have a desk with a printed itinerary and safety briefing.

**Group size:** Joiner tours are cheaper — PHP 800 to PHP 1,500 per person in most areas — but can be crowded. Private boats cost PHP 3,000 to PHP 6,000 per group and give you control over timing and stops. For families or non-swimmers, private is safer because the boatman can adjust.

**Itinerary timing:** Good operators leave early — 6:00 to 6:30 AM for dolphin watching and turtle spots, 8:30 to 9:30 AM for El Nido lagoons. Early departure means calmer seas and fewer boats at popular spots.

**What is included:** Ask if snorkel gear, life vest, lunch, environmental fees, and terminal fees are included. Many cheap quotes exclude fees that add PHP 200 to PHP 500 per person later.

## Tips Before You Go Island-Hopping

### Check PAGASA and Coast Guard advisories the night before

For Palawan, Bohol, and Siargao, a gale warning can cancel all small boats for a day. Have a backup land tour.

### Bring your own mask if you snorkel often

A mask that fits your face prevents leaks and fog. Test the seal by pressing it to your face without the strap — it should stay with a gentle inhale.

### Use reef-safe sunscreen, not regular sunscreen

Regular sunscreen with oxybenzone harms coral. Mineral-based reef-safe sunscreen or a rash guard protects both you and the reef.

### Keep cash in two places

Day money in a small wallet inside your dry bag, reserve in your hotel safe. Small bills for fees, larger bills for emergencies.

### Do not bring single-use plastic to islands

Many islands like Virgin Island, Balicasag, and Naked Island require you to bring trash back. Bring a reusable bottle and a small trash bag.

## So, What Should You Actually Pack?

Dry bag, rash guard, reef shoes, hat, reef-safe sunscreen, 1 liter water, towel, snorkel mask if you have one, phone in waterproof pouch, cash in small bills, seasickness tablets, and your hotel address on paper. On the boat, wear your life vest while moving, sit low, hydrate, and follow your guide's sanctuary rules.

With those basics, island-hopping stays what it should be — a day of turtles, sandbars, and lagoons, without hunting for gear or worrying about safety.
