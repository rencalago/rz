# Eat Like a Local at MassKara: A Bacolod Food Trail for Festival Week

**Meta description:** A relaxed food trail through Bacolod during MassKara Festival: what to eat, where to eat it, and how to pace yourself between the parades.

**Meta keywords:** MassKara Festival food, Bacolod food guide, Bacolod chicken inasal, piaya Bacolod, MassKara 2026 food, Bacolod food trip, things to eat in Bacolod

---

MassKara is famous for its masks, its music and its dancers, but ask anyone who has been and they will tell you the food is the other half of the story. Festival week fills the city with street stalls, late-night crowds and families who have been celebrating for weeks. If you plan your eating as carefully as your viewing spots, you will end each day full and happy.

## Start With the Dish Bacolod Is Known For

No Bacolod food trip is complete without chicken inasal. The chicken is marinated in local ingredients like calamansi, lemongrass and annatto, then grilled over charcoal and basted as it cooks, so the skin turns crisp and smoky while the meat stays juicy. It is usually served with rice, a side of soup and a spicy dipping sauce. Order it early in the day if you want the good pieces, since the best inasal spots tend to sell out by the evening parades.

Eat it sitting down if you can. Inasal is meant to be enjoyed slowly, and it is one of the simplest ways to feel like you have settled into the city rather than passing through it.

The marinade is what sets Bacolod's version apart from grilled chicken elsewhere in the Philippines. The annatto gives it a warm colour, and the calamansi keeps it bright. If you like to compare, order a second plate at a different stall and notice how the balance of smoke, sourness and salt changes from one place to the next. Many locals have a favourite and will be glad to explain why.

## Sweet Stops on the Walk

Bacolod's sugar heritage shows up in its sweets. Piaya, a thin flatbread filled with muscovado sugar, is the local treat to try. It is sold in many bakeries and at market stalls, and it makes an easy snack to carry as you walk between venues. Buy a few to share, since the sweetness is rich and a small piece goes a long way.

For something cooler, look for stalls selling local ice desserts and cold drinks. The afternoons get hot and humid during the festival, so a sweet, cold treat is a good way to reset before the evening crowds arrive.

## Plan Your Food Trail Around the Parades

The easiest way to eat well during MassKara is to plan around the schedule rather than trying to grab food at random. Eat a substantial lunch before heading to the day's events, since queues for food near the venues can be long once the parades begin. Keep a light snack in your bag for the walk back, because roads near the festival areas are often closed and it can take longer than expected to find a seat.

Give yourself one main meal per day and treat the rest as snacks. Festival food is often shared, and it is more fun to try three or four small plates with friends than to commit to one large order.

## Tips for Eating Well During the Crowds

Carry small bills, since many street stalls prefer cash. Bring water and keep it topped up, because the heat and the walking add up quickly. Stick to stalls with a steady line of customers, which usually means the food is turning over fast and is fresh. If something looks like it has been sitting out for a long time, skip it and try the next stall.

Wash your hands before eating, and keep wet wipes handy for sticky fingers. A good tip is to ask locals which stall they go to. Bacolod people are proud of their food, and most will happily point you to a favourite place.

## Eating Away From the Crowds

The busiest stalls sit right on the parade routes, and that is where the queues are longest. A few streets back from the main festival areas you will often find small family-run eateries that serve the same kinds of food at a slower pace. These are a good option for a quiet lunch between events, or for a sit-down meal when your feet need a break.

Check opening hours before you walk over, though. Many small places adjust their schedules during festival week, and some close early on parade nights. A quick message to the restaurant or a look at its social media page will save you a wasted trip. Morning markets are another good option for an early start, when the produce is fresh and the stalls are calm.

## A Simple One-Day Food Trail

Here is a relaxed way to structure a day. Start with a late breakfast of rice and a light meal, then have a proper chicken inasal lunch before the afternoon events. In the late afternoon, grab piaya and a cold drink as you walk. In the evening, share a few small plates while you watch the parade, and save room for one last sweet treat on the way home.

Plan for two or three food stops per day rather than trying to eat everywhere. The festival is long, and you will enjoy it more if you are not rushing between meals.

## What to Bring Home

Piaya makes one of the easiest gifts to take back to friends and family. Look for sealed packs from established bakeries, since sealed packaging keeps the flatbread fresher on the journey and makes it easier to pack in a bag. Ask the seller how long the pack will last before you buy, especially if you are travelling a long way. Muscovado sugar is another local souvenir that travels well, and it is a nice addition to a gift box if you want something a little different from the usual snacks.

## Final Thoughts

MassKara is a festival of smiles, and a good meal is one of the easiest ways to share in that. Eat slowly, ask for recommendations, and leave some room for something you did not plan to try. The best festival food is often the one you found by accident on a busy street corner. Bacolod's food rewards curiosity, so take a few detours, talk to the people behind the grills, and let your last meal of the trip be the one you think about on the flight home.
