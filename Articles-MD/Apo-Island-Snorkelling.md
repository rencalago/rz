# Apo Island Snorkelling

Apo Island makes snorkelling unusually easy. You step off the boat, walk a few meters from the beach, and you are already above coral and green sea turtles feeding on seagrass. The island is small — about 72 hectares and home to fewer than a thousand residents — but the reef around it supports more than 650 fish species and around 400 coral species, and it has been protected by the local community since 1982.

The draw is not just the turtles. The marine sanctuary on the southeastern side is a no-take zone where coral has had decades to recover. Visibility is often excellent in the dry months, and the water stays shallow enough for beginners while still offering depth for stronger swimmers. This guide covers where to snorkel, how to get there from Dumaguete, what it costs right now, and how to do it without harming the reef.

Here is what to expect, how to plan your day, and how to make the most of your time in the water.

## 1. Turtle Point — The Easiest Place to See Turtles

Turtle Point sits right off the main beach where boats land. The water is shallow, often knee to chest deep at high tide, with patches of seagrass and coral heads where green and hawksbill turtles graze. You do not need to swim far; many visitors spot their first turtle within minutes of entering.

A licensed guide is mandatory for this area. The guide keeps you in the allowed swimming corridor, reminds you to keep a distance of at least a few meters from turtles, and watches for people stepping on coral. It is a simple system that works — it keeps the turtles calm and the coral intact.

**Best for:** First-time snorkellers, families, and anyone who wants a high chance of seeing turtles without a long swim.

**What to do:** Enter slowly, stay horizontal, and let the guide point you toward feeding areas. Watch for cleaner wrasses riding on turtle shells — a good sign a turtle is relaxed and staying in one place.

**Location:** Off the main cove, southeastern side of Apo Island.
**Practical tip:** Go in the morning. Turtles feed closer to shore early, seas are usually calmer, and there are fewer boats.

## 2. Marine Sanctuary Area — Coral Gardens and Reef Fish

Just beyond Turtle Point, the Marine Sanctuary opens into denser coral gardens. This is where you see the broader reef — branching corals, massive boulder corals, schools of parrotfish, angelfish, clownfish, and occasional jacks passing through. The slope drops gradually, so you can stay shallow or move a little deeper if you are comfortable.

The sanctuary was established in 1982 with help from Silliman University marine biologist Dr. Angel Alcala, and the community itself enforces the rules. Fishing is prohibited inside the buoys, which is why fish density remains high even close to the village.

**Best for:** Snorkellers who already feel comfortable in open water and want more than just turtles.

**What to do:** Focus on buoyancy. Keep fins near the surface and avoid kicking up sand. If you wear a life vest, adjust it so you float without needing to stand.

**Location:** Southeastern reef, marked by buoys south of the main beach.
**Check before going:** Ask your guide where the current is moving that day; the sanctuary side can have a gentle drift.

## 3. Rock Point and Chapel — For Stronger Swimmers

On the eastern and northern sides, sites known to divers as Rock Point East, Rock Point West, and Chapel also work for experienced snorkellers on calm days. The coral walls are steeper, the water turns a deeper blue, and visibility can reach 20 to 30 meters in dry season. You are more likely to see larger schools here, but you also need to be honest about your fitness and comfort with currents.

These spots are not part of the standard turtle-guided swim. You usually reach them with a boat that stays with you, and you should only attempt them when your guide says conditions are suitable.

**Best for:** Confident swimmers and free divers who want dramatic reef walls.

**What to do:** Use fins, stay with your boatman, and keep swims short. These areas are best treated as a second swim after Turtle Point, not a replacement.

## How to Get to Apo Island from Dumaguete

Most trips start in Dumaguete City and go through Malatapay Wharf in Zamboanguita.

1. **Dumaguete to Malatapay:** Take a Ceres bus or jeepney from Robinsons Place Dumaguete or the Ceres terminal to Malatapay Market. The ride takes 30 to 40 minutes and costs around PHP 25 to 50. Tricycles to the terminal add PHP 50 to 100.

2. **Malatapay to Apo Island:** Banca boats leave on demand from 7 AM. There is no fixed ferry schedule. A small boat for up to 4 people is typically PHP 3,000 to PHP 3,500 roundtrip, larger boats for 6 to 8 people around PHP 5,000. Sharing with other travellers keeps the cost down. The crossing takes 30 to 45 minutes depending on sea conditions.

3. **Last boats off the island:** Boats generally stop leaving Apo Island between 2 PM and 4 PM for safety, earlier if waves pick up. Arrive at Malatapay before 9 AM to avoid cancellations, especially from November to February when Habagat seas can be rough.

If you are coming from Siquijor, you will need to ferry to Dumaguete first (about 45 minutes) then follow the same route. Some resorts in Dauin and Dumaguete offer day tours that bundle transport, guide, gear, and lunch.

## Current Fees and What to Budget

Fees fund reef monitoring, ranger salaries, and community programs. Bring cash — there are no ATMs on the island.

- **Island entrance / environmental fee:** PHP 100 for Filipino visitors, PHP 300 for foreign visitors. Some barangay notices list PHP 25 for Negros Oriental residents. Children under 6 are often free.
- **Snorkelling guide (mandatory for Turtle Point):** PHP 300 per group, usually up to 4 people, for the whole day.
- **Snorkelling gear rental:** Mask and snorkel around PHP 100 each, life vest around PHP 100. Full sets are sometimes PHP 200 to PHP 300 if fins are included.
- **Diving guide fee:** Around PHP 200 if you add a dive.
- **Boat:** PHP 3,000 to PHP 5,000 roundtrip per boat as above, split among passengers.

Tours from Dumaguete or Dauin that include van, boat, guide, gear, and lunch typically start around PHP 2,000 to PHP 3,000 per person when shared.

Check the board at the Malatapay tourism desk and the registration hut on Apo Island on the day you go, as posted rates are adjusted by the Protected Area Management Board.

## Sanctuary Rules You Need to Follow

Apo Island is managed under the National Integrated Protected Areas System. Rules are simple and enforced by local guides:

- Do not touch, chase, or block turtles. Stay at least 3 to 4 meters away.
- Do not step on coral or pick up anything from the seabed.
- Do not fish or bring spearfishing gear into sanctuary zones.
- Use reef-safe sunscreen or wear a rash guard instead.
- Bring your trash back to the mainland. Avoid single-use plastics.
- Follow the guide's corridor and do not swim outside the buoy line without permission.

These are not suggestions for photos — guides can end a swim if rules are broken.

## Tips Before You Snorkel Apo Island

### Bring your own gear if you can

Rental masks often fit poorly. A mask that seals well, a dry snorkel, and fins make the experience far more comfortable. If you rent, test the seal before leaving the shop.

### Wear a rash guard and reef shoes

A long-sleeve rash guard protects you from sun and minor scrapes better than sunscreen alone. Light reef shoes or booties help on the rocky entry, but you should still avoid standing on coral once in the water.

### Keep your body flat

The most common mistake is swimming vertically and kicking coral. Float horizontally, look down, and use slow fin kicks. If you feel tired, signal your guide and float on your back.

### Do not feed the fish

Feeding changes fish behavior and damages coral. Watch the natural feeding instead — turtles grazing seagrass, parrotfish scraping algae off coral.

### Plan around midday heat and crowds

Morning light is best for photography, and boats are fewer before 10 AM. Bring at least 1 liter of water, and keep your boat time flexible. If seas turn choppy after lunch, your boatman may advise an earlier return.

## So, Is Apo Island Snorkelling Worth It?

If your goal is an easy, reliable turtle encounter with genuinely healthy coral, Apo Island is hard to beat from Dumaguete. Turtle Point gives beginners a close-up look without a long boat ride to a reef, while the sanctuary and Rock Point offer more depth when you want it.

Keep expectations realistic. This is a small, low-key island with limited shops and basic facilities. Walking is the only way to get around, accommodations are simple — Mario's Homestay, Liberty Lodge, Apo Island Beach Resort, and a couple of others — and there is little to do beyond snorkelling, diving, and the short hikes to the lighthouse and Habagat View Deck. That simplicity is part of why the reef has survived.

For the best day, arrive early at Malatapay, share a small boat, hire the mandatory guide, snorkel Turtle Point first, then decide if conditions allow a second swim along the sanctuary. Take only photos, keep your distance, and confirm current fees at the gate before you pay.
