Driving to Baguio: Routes, Number Coding and Parking

Ren Calago 14 November 20260

A car changes what a Baguio trip feels like. You leave when you want, stop for strawberries or a toilet break without asking anyone, and carry the jackets, the pasalubong and the extra bag of groceries your family insists on bringing. You also take on a mountain climb, a stack of toll gates, a coding scheme most visitors only hear about on arrival, and a city where finding a parking slot on a Saturday can take longer than lunch.


From Metro Manila, most drivers head up NLEX, SCTEX and TPLEX, leave the expressway near Rosario in La Union, then pick one of the mountain roads into the city. Marcos Highway is the dependable main climb. Kennon Road is the old scenic way up and opens or closes depending on the weather and repairs. Naguilian Road makes the most sense if you are coming in from the La Union coast.


This guide walks through each road and when to use it, how the weekday number coding works for out-of-town plates, where to leave the car in the city centre, and the mountain-driving habits that keep the trip calm. Read it alongside the official advisories on the day you leave, because the roads change faster than any blog post.

Is it worth bringing the car?


It depends on what the car will actually do once you arrive. A private vehicle earns its keep on the trip up and on outings beyond the centre: La Trinidad, the northern attractions, or a loop further into Benguet. Inside the downtown grid it is often slower than your own feet. The streets are steep and narrow, intersections jam quickly, and every new stop means hunting for another slot.


Bring the car

Families, heavy luggage, side trips

Worth it if you are travelling as a group, carrying a lot, or planning days in La Trinidad and other parts of Benguet.

Park it and walk

A downtown-only weekend

If the plan is Session Road, Burnham Park, the cathedral and the market, the car will mostly sit in a lot. Walking and taxis are easier.

The sweet spot

A hotel with secure parking

Drive up, leave the car at your accommodation, and take it out only for trips beyond the city centre.


Manila to Baguio by car: the main route


The expressway half of the trip is the easy part. Signage is clear, the roads are wide, and the only real variable is traffic. The work starts once you leave TPLEX and the land begins to tilt upward.

  1. Step 1

    Head north on NLEX

    Leave Metro Manila on the North Luzon Expressway. An early start helps with city traffic, though it will not empty the road on a holiday.

  2. Step 2

    Switch to SCTEX toward Tarlac

    Follow the signs for the Subic–Clark–Tarlac Expressway and stay on it toward Tarlac.

  3. Step 3

    Ride TPLEX to the Rosario end

    Join the Tarlac–Pangasinan–La Union Expressway and stay on it until it ends at Rosario, La Union.

  4. Step 4

    Pick your way up the mountain

    Choose Marcos Highway, Kennon Road or another approach based on that day's road advisory, not on habit or a map app's suggestion.

About travel time: skip fixed estimates on long weekends, Holy Week, Christmas and Panagbenga. Queues can start well before the climb and carry on into the city.


Sorting out RFID and tolls


NLEX and SCTEX run on Easytrip, while TPLEX uses Autosweep. Interoperability rules and account requirements have changed before, so check what your vehicle needs before the trip and top up every account you will use. Your total toll depends on where you enter and exit, your vehicle class and the rates in force that month, so look them up on the operators' own channels rather than trusting an old cost breakdown.


Toll prep in five checks

  • Balance: load enough on each RFID account before you reach the first gate
  • Sticker: make sure it is properly installed and readable
  • Backup: have another way to pay in case a reader fails
  • Rates: look up current tolls on the official expressway channels
  • Rental car: ask the provider how tolls and RFID loads are handled

Which mountain road should you take?


Three roads get mentioned the most, plus a fourth that authorities sometimes point drivers to. They suit different situations, and the best one on paper is not always the one that is open, so let your starting point, your vehicle, the weather and the latest restrictions decide.


The main climb

Marcos Highway

Officially the Aspiras–Palispis Highway, from the La Union side

The sensible default for most drivers. It is a principal paved route with a steadier climb than Kennon, which makes it the usual pick when Kennon is restricted. Buses and trucks use it too, so expect slow uphill stretches on busy days, along with long curves, fog in places and the occasional lane closure for slope work.

Watch for: steep curves, poor visibility in rain, road slips and heavy vehicles

The scenic one

Kennon Road

Rosario area to Baguio, past the Lion's Head

The historic road up, with forest views and the Lion's Head landmark along the way. When it is open it can be a shorter approach from the Rosario side. It is also the road most exposed to rockfalls and landslides, and access has been limited many times for safety and repairs. In late August 2026, DPWH-CAR reopened it to light vehicles with a five-ton load limit, and warned that non-residents could be turned back during continuous heavy rain. Take that as history, not as a promise about your travel date.

Watch for: closures, vehicle limits, one-lane repair sections and rockfall after rain

From the coast

Naguilian Road

Baguio–Bauang Road, from Bauang, La Union

A good fit if you are pairing Baguio with a few days on the La Union coast, or if the other roads are jammed or restricted. The climb is long and winding with plenty of elevation changes, so it is not automatically quicker. Give yourself extra time if you have never driven it.

Watch for: blind curves, long descents that need a low gear, shifting visibility

When advised

Asin–Nangalisan–San Pascual Road

Alternative connection to the La Union boundary

Road advisories sometimes name this route for vehicles that should stay off restricted parts of Kennon Road. Use it when authorities tell you to, after checking its passability and any limits on your vehicle. It is not a shortcut to try just because a map shows a thinner line.

Watch for: current passability and vehicle restrictions in the latest advisory

The simple rule: take whichever road the authorities say is safe and passable on the day you drive. A shorter scenic route stops being a good idea the moment there is a closure, a landslide warning or a load limit your vehicle breaks.


Baguio number coding for visitors


Baguio runs its own weekday coding scheme, and it covers visiting cars as well as local ones. A plate from outside Benguet does not get a pass. The schedule below is the one posted on the city's official VISITA tourism portal.

Where the coding applies


Not every road in the city. The restriction covers a designated coding zone, and the VISITA portal publishes a map showing the zone, its perimeter roads and the entry points. Perimeter roads can be treated differently from streets inside the zone, so a friend's description or a screenshot from last year is not good enough. Open the current map on the Baguio VISITA advisories page and trace the exact streets you plan to drive.


Are tourists exempt?


No. The city's tourism portal says so directly. A hotel booking, a tourist registration or an out-of-town plate does not give you permission to drive inside the zone on your coding day.


What to do if your plate is coded on travel day


  • Arrive outside coding hoursIf your schedule allows, enter before 7:00 AM or after 7:00 PM. Arriving early does not let you keep driving in the zone once enforcement starts.
  • Leave the car at your hotelBook a place with confirmed parking, then walk or ride taxis to the central sights for the day.
  • Spend the day outside the zoneOnly after checking the official map and confirming your whole route stays out of the restricted area.
  • Move your downtown dayIf the itinerary is flexible, schedule your central Baguio driving for a day your plate is free.

Where to park in Baguio's city centre


Parking is where most driving trips to Baguio go sideways. The centre pulls in shoppers, commuters, residents and visitors all at once, and there is not much road to share. The calmest approach is to choose a lot close to the area you want to see, park once, and do the rest on foot. Posted rates and hours change, so read the sign at the entrance before you commit.


Park and lagoon

Burnham Park pay parking

Around Burnham Park and the Athletic Bowl

Public pay parking around the park, including Burnham Pay Parking, puts you within walking distance of the lake, nearby restaurants and the downtown streets. Slots near the park go quickly on weekends and holidays, so have a second option in mind instead of idling in a queue that blocks traffic behind you.

Best for: a day around Burnham Park and the nearby downtown area

Mall parking

SM City Baguio

Luneta Hill, near Upper Session Road

A paid facility serving the mall, handy if you plan to shop, eat or use the mall as a jumping-off point for Upper Session Road. Follow the marked vehicle entrance and exit, and check the posted rates, payment rules and closing time as you go in.

Best for: mall visits, meals and errands on Upper Session Road

Multi-level

Joem Parking Building

Mabini Street, city centre

A parking building in the middle of town, a short walk from Session Road, Baguio Cathedral and the shops around them. Confirm its hours before you leave the car, especially if dinner might run late.

Best for: parking near the central business district and walking from there

Near the church

Baguio Cathedral pay parking

Fr. Carlu Street

Paid parking near the cathedral works well for a visit to the church and short walks around downtown. Ask the attendant about the rate and whether the area is meant for visitors or churchgoers, because neighbouring lots can follow different rules.

Best for: cathedral visits and a quick loop around the centre

Overflow

Kisad Road and Jose Abad Santos Drive

Edges of Burnham Park and the Athletic Bowl

More pay parking sits along these roads, useful when the obvious Burnham lots are full. Navigate to the actual vehicle entrance, since a map pin may only mark the general area, and expect fewer slots during events.

Best for: drivers happy to walk a few extra minutes to the park or downtown

Parking habits that save you trouble


  • Ask the hotel firstIs parking included, paid, first come first served, or reserved? Will it fit your vehicle?
  • Check overnight rulesFind out whether you can leave the car overnight and when you must pick it up.
  • Keep cash handyNot every lot takes digital payment.
  • Read the sign, keep the ticketCheck the posted rate before you enter, not after you exit.
  • Hide your thingsNo bags, gadgets or valuables left in view.
  • Skip the "free" kerbAn empty roadside space is not always legal. Stay clear of sidewalks, crossings, intersections and no-parking signs.

Drive around town or take taxis?


For most visitors the answer is both, at different times. The central district is compact enough to walk if you do not mind slopes and a change in the weather, and taxis fill the gaps when a destination is farther out or the hill is too steep. That way you are not hunting for a new slot at every stop.


Your car

The trip up and the side trips

Use it for the drive to Baguio and for places outside the central district.

Your feet

The downtown cluster

Burnham Park, Session Road, the cathedral and the market sit close enough to link on foot, depending on where you parked.

A taxi

Far stops and bad weather

Flag one when the next stop is across town, the climb is steep, or the rain sets in.

Best time to make the drive


Leaving Metro Manila early helps you dodge some city traffic, but it is no guarantee of a quiet expressway or a clear mountain road. What matters more is which day you pick.


Weekdays

The easiest days

Ordinary weekdays outside holidays and big events are kinder for driving and parking. Just check which day your plate is coded before you plan a downtown day.

Weekends and holidays

Expect company

Friday afternoons, Saturdays and the Sunday drive home get busy, more so in the cool months and on long weekends. Book a room with confirmed parking early, keep dinner reservations loose, and plan to leave the car at the hotel for most of the stay.

Rainy season

Check twice

Heavy rain raises the risk of landslides, rockfall, road slips and sudden closures. Look at the advisories before you leave home and again before you start the climb.


Mountain driving habits that keep the trip calm


Nobody wins anything by rushing the road to Baguio. Even confident drivers do better when they slow down and drive to the terrain.


Six habits for the climb and the drive back down.

01 Before you go

Get the car checked

Brakes, tyres, lights, wipers, coolant and battery. The brakes matter most, since the long descent works them hard.

02 Descents

Let the gears do the work

Drop to a lower gear on long downhill stretches instead of riding the brakes. If you smell burning or the pedal feels wrong, pull over only at a safe, marked spot.

03 Curves

No overtaking on blind bends

Stay in your lane. Trucks and buses swing wide on curves, and oncoming cars appear with little warning.

04 Fog

Low beams, not high

Turn your lights on so others can see you in rain and fog, but skip the high beams. They bounce off thick fog and make it worse.

05 Spacing

Leave a bigger gap

Wet roads and downhill grades make hard braking harder. Give the car ahead more room than you would in the lowlands.

06 Closures

Trust the barricade over the app

Navigation apps can miss a fresh closure. Follow road signs, traffic enforcers and official advisories, and save your photos for proper viewpoints, not narrow shoulders.


Before you leave home


The night before

  • Road advisoriesCheck DPWH-CAR for Kennon Road, Marcos Highway, Naguilian Road and any alternative routes, and confirm your chosen road is open to your vehicle.
  • WeatherLook at the forecast for the mountain areas, not just Manila.
  • CodingMatch your plate to the weekday schedule and open the current coding-zone map on the VISITA portal.
  • Parking and tollsConfirm hotel parking, then check RFID balances and current toll requirements.
  • The car and the cabinInspect tyres, brakes, lights, wipers and fluids. Pack a phone charger, drinking water and basic emergency supplies.
  • Offline backupsSave your accommodation address and a second parking option for when the signal drops.

Official pages worth bookmarking


  • Baguio VISITA tourism portalThe city's official visitor site, with the number coding zone map.
  • Baguio City traffic monitoringRoad conditions, incidents and alternative routes inside the city.
  • Department of Public Works and HighwaysLook for DPWH-CAR updates on the mountain roads before you leave.

Is driving to Baguio a good idea?


Yes, if you want flexibility, you are bringing family, or your plans stretch beyond the city centre. The drive is very manageable with a checked car, a route picked from that day's advisory and enough slack in the schedule for the climb.


Two things deserve the most planning: the state of the mountain roads and the coding scheme. Kennon Road is lovely when the authorities say it is open to your vehicle, and only then. Marcos Highway and Naguilian Road are solid alternatives that still need a look at current conditions. Once in the city, a hotel with secure parking does more for your mood than any shortcut, so park, walk the centre, and save the car for the trips that need it.
